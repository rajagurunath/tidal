\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Commercial LLM providers sell two products from the same weights: online inference,
priced for latency, and a batch API priced at half the online rate with a 24-hour
completion window. Self-hosted deployments have no equivalent of the second product,
even though a decode-dominated engine iteration uses a small fraction of its
per-iteration token budget. Research systems that co-locate offline work behind online
traffic (HyGen, ConServe) recover much of this idle capacity, but none of them exposes
what makes the batch product sellable: a customer-facing completion contract. We present
Tidal, which adds a \emph{contracted} batch tier to vLLM: an OpenAI-wire-compatible
\code{/v1/batches} front end with durable storage; per-job \textbf{laxity-driven
escalation} (classical Least-Laxity-First, applied per batch job against an online tier)
that leaves on-track jobs at the lowest priority band and escalates at-risk jobs early,
continuously, and independently; a token-bucket progress floor; and a laxity-based
\textbf{admission feasibility check} that rejects deadlines the system's own arithmetic
cannot meet. We implement the same policy at two placements --- an external gateway over
an unmodified engine, and an in-engine work-conserving scheduler deployed as a plugin
(not a fork) whose interference model calibrates itself from live step timings with no
offline profiling --- and compare them on identical traces, a design axis prior systems
do not evaluate. On a deliberately modest CPU testbed we validate the mechanisms and find
a placement \emph{inversion}: the gateway holds online p99 request latency to
$1.76\times$ baseline where the in-engine scheduler reaches $8.25\times$, because the
binding interference channel (resident-decode contention) sits below the reach of
admission-time control --- while the in-engine scheduler harvests 71\% of the offline
throughput ceiling versus the gateway's 45\%, making placement a throughput/latency
frontier rather than a ranking. A subsequent GPU validation (RTX A6000, Qwen2.5-7B, rented
on the io.net marketplace) finds the gateway harvesting \textbf{69.3\% of the same node's
steady-state offline ceiling at $1.18\times$/$1.23\times$ online p50/p99 under flat load,
and 78.1\% at ${\sim}1.13\times$/$1.21\times$ under a diurnal trace (latency ratios cross-node)}, with
per-minute batch completions anti-correlating with online load at $r = -0.85$ --- the same
tide-filling the CPU testbed shows at $-0.95$. Those GPU figures are post-correction: an
independent numerical audit rejected our first ceiling (a probe average that co-serving
runs ``exceeded''), and we report the methodology point as a result in its own right,
since any co-serving figure above 100\% is a tell that the denominator is not a ceiling.
We are explicit about what the testbed cannot show,
including that no tested load regime discriminated the escalation machinery end-to-end.
The implementation, including a client that plans coding work onto batch APIs, is open
source.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The economics of LLM serving are dominated by GPU hours, and GPU hours are dominated by
waste. An engine serving interactive chat traffic runs its scheduler every few tens of
milliseconds; in each iteration it may decode one token for each of a few dozen live
requests while its compute budget --- the number of tokens the hardware could process in
that same iteration at acceptable latency --- sits in the thousands. Decode is
memory-bandwidth-bound: the weights stream from HBM whether the iteration carries 20
tokens or 2{,}000. The marginal cost of adding prefill work to a decode-heavy iteration
is small until a compute knee, a fact exploited by chunked-prefill
schedulers~\citep{sarathi} and by every co-location system since.

Providers monetize this slack directly: submit a file of requests, receive results within
24 hours, pay half the online price. A self-hosted deployment running vLLM cannot offer
this product. vLLM ships an offline batch runner that requires dedicating the machine and
an online server with no batch-job surface; a proposal to add SLA tiers to its scheduler
was closed without adoption~\citep{vllm-rfc-30256}.

The research literature has a different gap. HyGen~\citep{hygen} and
ConServe~\citep{conserve} co-locate offline requests behind online traffic with strict
priority and preemption, recovering 78--88\% of dedicated-offline throughput while
protecting online SLOs --- and both include mechanisms that temper offline starvation.
What neither exposes is a \emph{customer-facing completion contract}: a specific job, a
specific deadline, admission that refuses infeasible work, and scheduling that spends
exactly as much online headroom as that deadline requires. Deadline-aware LLM schedulers
exist --- Niyama~\citep{niyama} co-schedules QoS classes with per-request deadlines and
claims QoS targets --- but at second-to-minute horizons, with deadlines as optimization
targets and graceful degradation under overload. The batch-API semantics that OpenAI and
Anthropic actually sell --- day-scale windows, per-job, with an admission decision ---
appear in none of them.

The contract is the product. A customer submits an overnight batch because ``done by
morning'' is dependable enough to plan around. Our motivating client,
lazycode~\citep{lazycode} --- a coding agent that plans work with a realtime model and
executes it on provider batch APIs at the discount --- exists only because that window is
dependable. Making self-hosted batch serving sellable requires promoting the deadline
from an optimization target to a contract with an admission face and a scheduling face.

Tidal implements both faces with deliberately classical machinery. Per batch job, the
system tracks \emph{laxity} --- time remaining minus work remaining at the observed
completion rate. At \textbf{admission}, a job whose projected completion exceeds its
window (with margin) is refused outright --- the self-hosted analogue of commercial APIs
rejecting oversized jobs. In \textbf{scheduling}, urgency ramps only inside a short
escalation horizon of remaining slack: an on-track job never rises above the lowest
priority band, no matter how long it has been running (we document how easily this
property is silently lost: our first implementation scaled urgency by the full window,
reintroducing wall-clock aging --- a healthy job at hour 12 escalated for no reason; an
adversarial cross-model review caught it, and a regression test now pins the hour-12
case). An at-risk job escalates early, continuously, and independently of other jobs,
avoiding synchronized floods; at maximum urgency it sits one notch below online priority
with a token-bucket floor guaranteeing forward progress --- the floor also closes the
loop, since guaranteed progress raises laxity, stabilizing an escalated job at the floor
rather than oscillating. A configuration flag (\code{sla\_strict}) allows true priority
parity at the deadline; we treat strictness as a policy dial and leave measuring its
latency price to future work. Least-Laxity-First is sixty years old~\citep{llf};
FREESH~\citep{freesh} has already run LLF inside an LLM engine for energy-aware
scheduling of a single request class. What is new here is narrower and, we argue, more
useful: LLF applied \emph{per batch job against an online tier}, carrying commercial
batch-window semantics, with the same laxity arithmetic reused as the admission test.

We implement the policy at two placements and compare them. \emph{Technique A} places all
policy in a sidecar gateway: batch items are injected as low-priority requests into an
unmodified vLLM, throttled by additive-increase/multiplicative-decrease control over the
engine's public metrics. It tracks upstream vLLM releases with no coupling beyond the
OpenAI API and \code{/metrics}. \emph{Technique B} moves admission into the engine via
vLLM's pluggable scheduler interface (a non-stable interface; we pin a version): a
subclass wraps the stock scheduler, fills each iteration's residual token budget with
batch work, bounds interference with a latency model in ConServe's context-aware
form~\citep{conserve} but fitted continuously from the engine's own step timings,
reserves KV-cache headroom for online bursts, and preempts the cheapest victim first.
Neither placement forks vLLM.

Contributions:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Batch-API contract semantics for a co-located tier}: per-job day-scale
    deadlines with laxity-driven escalation, a progress floor, and laxity-based admission
    --- the customer-facing contract that co-location systems do not expose and
    deadline-aware schedulers do not target.
  \item \textbf{A self-calibrating interference model}: technique B fits its step-latency
    model from live (tokens, context, time) observations with an $R^2$-gated fallback,
    removing the offline profiling sweep HyGen and ConServe require.
  \item \textbf{The placement comparison}: the same policy externally vs in-engine on
    identical traces, revealing a throughput/latency frontier and an inversion the
    fork-based literature --- which evaluates only its own placement --- has not reported.
    On rented A6000 GPUs the gateway then harvests 69.3\% (flat load, at
    $1.18\times$/$1.23\times$ online p50/p99) to 78.1\% (diurnal, at ${\sim}1.13\times$/$1.21\times$)
    of the same node's \emph{steady-state} dedicated-offline ceiling (latency ratios cross-node): the median co-location tax that costs $2.54\times$ on the CPU box costs
    $1.18\times$ there, while the harvest itself lands in the band the co-location
    literature reports.
  \item \textbf{Deployability artifacts}: durable OpenAI-wire API, crash recovery,
    idempotent state machines, metering, and a real client --- with the operational
    semantics (\cref{sec:impl}) a paper usually omits and an operator cannot.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Motivation}

\subsection{One budget, two products}

A vLLM V1 iteration schedules up to \code{max\_num\_batched\_tokens} ($\tau$) tokens
across running and waiting requests; $\tau$ is sized so an iteration's latency fits the
inter-token target. A running decode consumes one token per iteration; a prefill consumes
up to a chunk. The KV cache --- a separate budget --- bounds how many requests may be
resident. The token budget is the perishable good: unused tokens in an iteration are gone.

\subsection{Why best-effort is not a product}

Consider a large batch submitted against a deployment that then runs at sustained high
online load. Best-effort co-location delivers whatever slack happens to exist; the
customer learns at hour 24 how much that was. A wall-clock aging rule --- ``priority rises
every hour, parity at hour 24'' --- fails twice: it escalates small on-track jobs
pointlessly and escalates large at-risk jobs too late (full service beginning exactly when
time has run out). Feasibility is a relation between remaining work and remaining capacity
--- which is laxity, and which is why the same arithmetic serves as the admission test: a
job whose laxity is negative at submission should not be accepted at all.

\subsection{The placement question}

Engine schedulers act per iteration ($\sim$10--100\,ms) with exact visibility of the token
budget and KV state; an external controller acts at 1\,Hz on aggregate metrics. Intuition
says in-engine must dominate. But the engine's own priority preemption already covers
token-granularity interference between controller ticks, and an external controller
survives engine upgrades untouched. No prior co-location work isolates this variable; we
measure it, and the intuition does not survive contact (\cref{sec:eval}).

\section{Design}

\subsection{System overview}

Batch jobs enter through an OpenAI-wire-compatible \code{/v1/files} $+$ \code{/v1/batches}
API; any OpenAI SDK works unchanged with a different \code{base\_url}. Jobs are validated
line-by-line, checked for admission feasibility (\cref{sec:contract}), and stored durably
(SQLite WAL by default; the store is a repository interface, so Postgres is a DSN change).
Items progress \code{pending} $\rightarrow$ \code{inflight} $\rightarrow$
\code{succeeded}/\code{failed} with idempotent terminal transitions. Online traffic does
not traverse the gateway.

\subsection{The contract: laxity at admission and in scheduling}
\label{sec:contract}

For each active batch $b$ with deadline $D_b$, remaining items $R_b$, and observed
completion rate $r_b$ (sliding window; when no completion history exists the drain term is
dropped --- jobs escalate on evidence or deadline proximity, never on a missing
denominator):
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{laxity}(b)   &= (D_b - \mathrm{now}) - R_b / r_b \\
  \mathrm{urgency}(b)  &= \mathrm{clamp}\!\left(1 - \frac{\mathrm{laxity}(b)}{H},\,0,\,1\right)
    && H = \text{escalation horizon } (6\,\mathrm{h} \ll 24\,\mathrm{h}\ \text{window}) \\
  \mathrm{priority}(b) &= 100 \rightarrow 1 \ \text{linearly with urgency}
    && (\text{online} = 0;\ \code{sla\_strict}\ \text{allows}\ 0\ \text{at urgency}\ 1)
\end{align}

\Cref{fig:priority} plots the resulting curve.

\paragraph{Admission.} At \code{POST /v1/batches}, the projected completion
$R/r_{\mathrm{global}}$ is checked against the window with a configurable margin;
infeasible jobs are rejected with an error naming the arithmetic. No history $\rightarrow$
accept (the system cannot judge).

\paragraph{Scheduling.} An on-track job ($\mathrm{laxity} \ge H$) never rises above
priority 100 --- it exerts no \emph{escalation} pressure whatever the clock says.
(Co-location itself has a latency price even at the lowest band; \cref{sec:eval} measures
it. The escalation property is about never spending \emph{more} online headroom than the
deadline requires, not about zero cost.) The horizon, not the window, scales urgency ---
scaling by the window is wall-clock aging in disguise. Escalated jobs climb independently;
above urgency 0.9 a token bucket guarantees a minimum admission rate; the floor's
guaranteed progress feeds back into laxity, stabilizing the job at the floor. Escalation
applies at submission time (vLLM fixes a request's priority at admission); the backlog,
where laxity lives, is by construction the unsubmitted portion.

\subsection{Technique A: external control}
\label{sec:tech-a}

A 1\,Hz loop scrapes three public metrics, smooths them (EWMA), and adjusts a target
in-flight batch count by AIMD: halve on detected online queueing (conservatively --- the
waiting metric is not priority-partitioned, so we assume all our in-flight items are
queued) or high KV watermark; increment below a low watermark. Items are claimed in
deadline order, grouped by shared prompt prefix within a batch to recover some prefix-cache
locality through arrival order (we have not ablated how much of HyGen's engine-side
prefix-sharing benefit~\citep{hygen} this recovers), and submitted at their batch's current
laxity priority. Engine unavailability opens a circuit; in-flight items re-queue
idempotently. \Cref{fig:lifecycle-a} traces one batch through the whole path.

\input{fig-lifecycle-a}

\subsection{Technique B: in-engine work conservation}
\label{sec:tech-b}

\code{TidalScheduler} subclasses the V1 scheduler behind \code{--scheduler-cls} and wraps
--- never reimplements --- the stock \code{schedule()}. Requests with priority $\ge 1$ are
the batch class (online is 0 alone; an \code{sla\_strict} job that reaches 0 is
deliberately treated as online --- that \emph{is} parity). The pre-gate hides batch waiting
requests beyond a per-iteration cap $X$ from the stock pass (restored exception-safely),
where $X$ solves $\hat{T}(P_{\mathrm{on}} + x, C) \le \mathrm{TBT}_{\mathrm{budget}}$ with
$\hat{T}(P,C) = k_1 P + k_2 P(P+C) + k_4 (P+C) + k_5$ --- ConServe's context-aware form,
which prices the attention cost a pure token count misses. Coefficients fit continuously
from the engine's own $(P, C, \text{step-time})$ stream: ring buffer, periodic least
squares, non-negativity clamps, an $R^2$ gate with a conservative static fallback. With
zero online requests resident, the cap is lifted entirely (offline mode: fill $\tau$). A KV
guardband (EWMA of online demand, clamped 5--30\%) stops batch admission before online
bursts can be starved of blocks; proactive eviction picks the batch victim with the least
non-prefix-cached work. Enforcement is at chunk granularity --- we bound which requests the
stock scheduler sees, not its inner token loop --- which is what keeps the wrapper robust to
upstream drift (it survived a 1{,}682-commit jump during development) at the cost
\cref{sec:eval} quantifies. \Cref{fig:lifecycle-b} shows one iteration.

\input{fig-lifecycle-b}

\subsection{What we deliberately did not build}

ConServe's layer-wise safepoint preemption and incremental KV checkpointing address
unbounded iteration times; chunked prefill bounds ours to one $\tau$-sized step. Both are
natural extensions where $\tau$-bounded iterations are still long, and \cref{sec:eval}'s
placement result argues sub-iteration control is exactly what technique B lacks.

\section{Implementation and operational semantics}
\label{sec:impl}

Tidal is a Python implementation over unmodified upstream vLLM (v0.26 series), with 424
tests, including integration tests that drive a live engine; the API layer is wire-verified
against the official OpenAI SDK. The operational semantics reviewers should hold us to:
\textbf{idempotency} --- item claims are single atomic updates; terminal item transitions
are idempotent, so a duplicate or late result never drifts \code{request\_counts};
result-file attachment is a first-writer-wins claim, so concurrent finalization cannot
split a batch's outputs. \textbf{Crash recovery} --- a restarted dispatcher re-queues
resultless in-flight items; re-execution rides vLLM's prefix cache. \textbf{Late results}
for cancelled or expired items are recorded-then-discarded without corrupting counts, and
un-wedge finalization. \textbf{Failure model} --- one dispatcher per store is assumed;
there is no ownership lease (a second dispatcher is safe for data, not for duplicated work
--- documented, with the lease as roadmap). \textbf{Metering} prices batch tokens at a
configurable discount against an online price table; we deliberately make no claim about
what discount a marginal-cost model would justify.

The client side is lazycode, whose Tidal adapter mirrors its Anthropic batch adapter
nearly line-for-line --- the strongest evidence for the wire-compatibility claim: an
existing batch-API client adopted the self-hosted tier as just another provider, with the
only remaining friction inherited from the OpenAI Batch wire itself. \Cref{fig:loop}
closes the loop from client intent to applied code; the client's architecture --- the
planning algebra, rewrite-rule optimizer, wave scheduler, and batch-vs-cached-realtime
economics --- is the subject of the companion paper~\citep{lazycode-paper} and is not
duplicated here. An engine plugin also has an observability
obligation a fork does not: vLLM's logging configuration attaches handlers only to its own
logger tree, and scheduler telemetry is emitted from a forked EngineCore process ---
technique B injects a logging configuration or runs blind.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Setup.} Apple M4 Pro, CPU-only vLLM build (v0.26.1 series @ \code{e6d67fdd}),
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, $\tau = 2048$, \code{--scheduling-policy priority},
\code{--enforce-eager}. Online load: seeded Poisson arrivals of short chat requests
(identical schedules across conditions); we measure total non-streaming \textbf{request
latency} (not TTFT/TBT --- on this configuration a request is a short prefill plus a short
decode). Batch: jobs of short chat completions. \textbf{Scope disclaimer}: CPU-scale
numbers characterize mechanisms and their relative behavior under one hardware regime. They
support no claims about GPU efficiency, absolute capacity, or production latency; the
planned GPU evaluation (8$\times$H200) is where those claims must be earned.

\paragraph{Q3 --- the contract, honestly.} We attempted to demonstrate end-to-end deadline
discrimination --- a feasible-but-tight job that best-effort co-location misses and laxity
escalation meets --- and did not find a load regime on this testbed that discriminates. At
3\,req/s with a 900-item/15-minute job, the batch completed with 3.5 minutes to spare even
under the heaviest online load we could apply (online p99 5.8\,s); at 2--2.6\,req/s, every
configuration completed early for both arms. On this hardware, feasible cases were easy for
best-effort AIMD and we could not construct a feasible-but-tight case: \textbf{we therefore
do not claim demonstrated deadline improvement.} What the runs do establish: the escalation
machinery operates (per-tick priority traces show laxity-driven bands engaging), the floor
engages at the configured urgency, and the admission check (\cref{sec:contract}) refuses
jobs the observed rate cannot serve. Constructing the discriminating regime --- bursty
diurnal online load with genuinely scarce slack --- is a primary goal of the GPU evaluation.

\paragraph{Q4 --- placement, and an inversion.} The naive expectation --- in-engine must
dominate --- is wrong on this hardware. Technique B's cap and guardband address interference
channels that never bind here: peak KV usage in every run was below 0.5\%, so guardband
eviction never fired, and a resident batch request decodes every iteration regardless of the
admission cap. The dominant interference channel on this box is resident-decode contention,
which only sub-iteration participation control could bound (\cref{sec:limitations}).
Technique A holds latency ($1.76\times$ vs $8.25\times$ at equal work; $1.97\times$ vs
$9.9\times$ at capacity) because its control point --- how many batch requests exist at all
--- is upstream of residency; B converts that residency into 26 more points of ceiling
instead. We expect the frontier to shift, and possibly invert back, on hardware where
compute rather than residency binds; measuring that crossover is the placement question's
real test. To our knowledge no prior work reports this comparison; the fork-based literature
evaluates only its own placement.

\paragraph{Q5 --- self-calibration.} From cold start, $\hat{T}$ passed its $R^2$ gate within
the first capped phase of the integration run and settled at MAPE $\approx 0.11$ on live
traffic; the batch-token cap $X$ moved from the cold-start 40 to a converged 1 --- correctly
tight, since at a 60\,ms budget with $\sim$25\,ms online-only steps this box has little slack
to sell, and the model discovered that (\cref{fig:timeline}). Regime-change adaptation (model
swap, thermal drift) is untested (\cref{sec:limitations}).

denominator is not a ceiling}, and we record the point as a result because the mistake is

ceiling is the probe's \textbf{steady-state tail} --- output tokens completed in the last
10\,s of the burst: 2049.6 / 2031.5 / 2057.0 tok/s, a $\pm 0.62\%$ cross-node band, which
makes it a tighter instrument as well as a correct one. Every ``\% of ceiling'' figure
below uses it; the whole-burst averages remain in \code{results/gpu\_figures/} for
traceability, labeled ramp-dominated. Throughput is normalized per node. \textbf{Latency
ratios, by contrast, are cross-node}: no within-node \code{online\_only} /
\code{technique\_a} pair exists in the evidence, so we label the ratios as cross-node
rather than implying a paired comparison.

have started to climb, together with the evidence status of each rung --- A0 is what
\cref{sec:gpu} measures on GPU; A-F and A-P are proven locally; A-D is design plus a metric
mapping.

The ladder is the complement of technique B, not a competitor to it. Technique B is
\emph{maximum visibility over minimum scope}: it sees one engine's iteration exactly. The
A-ladder trades that for coarser signals over widening scope, and ends at a decision B
cannot express at all --- parking work in time. As the one-liner:
\emph{``disaggregation separates prefill from decode in space; a deadline-contracted batch
tier separates them in time.''}

\paragraph{A-F: fleet placement (local experiment).} Two CPU replicas (Mac M4 Pro,
thread-count isolation \emph{only} --- macOS cannot pin cores, so shared memory bandwidth is
a named confound), phase-shifted diurnal online load (period 600\,s, phases $0$ and $\pi$),
20-minute window, seed 7, 4000-item pool identical in both arms. \textbf{This is not the
GPU setup of \cref{sec:gpu}} and its numbers are not comparable to it: Qwen2.5-0.5B, peak
1\,req/s, 16-token online completions --- a deliberately small rig whose only job is to
show the placement mechanism.
The arms are \code{placement=fleet} versus \code{placement=pinned} (all batch
$\rightarrow$ replica 0).
Harvest: fleet 2494 in-window items (32.2\,tok/s) versus pinned 1884 (24.4) $=$
\textbf{$+32\%$}. The mechanism is visible per replica: $\mathrm{corr}$(a replica's own
analytic online rate, its batch completions per 2\,min) $=$ \textbf{$-0.83$ (replica 1),
$-0.56$ (replica 2)}, with batch volume split roughly evenly (1239 / 1255) but \emph{timed}
into each replica's trough. The cost is real and we do not hide it: blended online p50
3.41\,s (fleet) versus 1.44\,s (pinned), p99 12.1 versus 3.7 --- attributed to the
shared-substrate confound (spreading batch loads \emph{both} engines' shared memory bus;
pinning sacrifices one replica and spares the other). GPU fleets with real isolation are
where the latency side resolves; the mechanism claim (placement follows slack) is what the
CPU experiment proves. Implementation: \code{fleet.py} placement (sticky prefix affinity
$+$ least-loaded $+$ headroom filter), per-replica AIMD with replica-local circuits, 95 new
tests (424 total), production-wired behind \code{TIDAL\_VLLM\_REPLICAS}.

\paragraph{A-P: freeze/thaw, a spike.}%
{\sloppy
If the batch tier may defer work, it may also
split a request's \emph{phases} across time. We ran the smallest experiment that could falsify this:
vLLM CPU build, \code{ExampleConnector} (the renamed \code{SharedStorageConnector} --- the
only device-agnostic in-tree connector), \code{kv\_role=kv\_both}, prefix caching disabled.
Prefill in process 1 $\rightarrow$ KV persisted to disk (23.6\,MB for a 1973-token prompt:
only whole 128-token blocks persist, so 1920 cached tokens at exactly 12{,}288\,B/token at
0.5B) $\rightarrow$ process killed $\rightarrow$ a fresh process thaws
$\rightarrow$ \textbf{bit-identical greedy decode}, with TTFT 7.277\,s $\rightarrow$
0.229\,s $=$ \textbf{$31.8\times$} (median of 5). A duplicate arm that thawed from the
in-engine prefix cache rather than across a process boundary measured $48.2\times$; we
quote the smaller cross-process number as primary because it is the one that demonstrates
\emph{durability} --- surviving process death is the property a deadline-deferred prefill
actually needs. Short prompts (208 tokens) get only $1.9\times$ (just 128 of the 208
tokens are cached, plus fixed per-file read costs), so the $\approx$1k-token crossover is
an \emph{interpolation} between two measured points, not a measured crossover.
Caveats, all of them load-bearing: a debug-grade connector; all-or-nothing
exact-prompt-hash keying
(no partial prefix reuse --- that is LMCache~\citep{lmcache} / Mooncake~\citep{mooncake}
territory); \textbf{total FLOPs are unchanged --- only their placement in time moves}; at
70B-class KV bytes/token this is $\sim$2 orders worse, making storage the binding economic
constraint; and GPU KV-connector mode forces piecewise cudagraphs.\par}

\paragraph{Where this sits in the literature.} DistServe, Splitwise and
Dynamo~\citep{distserve,splitwise,dynamo} disaggregate phases in \emph{space}, with the KV
transferred immediately from prefill worker to decode worker; A-P adds the \emph{temporal}
axis --- the same split, but with an arbitrary gap in between, which is only a sane thing to
do when a deadline says it is. LMCache and Mooncake~\citep{lmcache,mooncake} have solved the
KV-persistence \emph{mechanics} we are deliberately not rebuilding (partial prefix reuse,
tiered stores, transfer engines); what we add on top is the \emph{deadline economics} that
decides when parking a prefill is worth its storage bill. Llumnix~\citep{llumnix}
rebalances online requests across instances by live migration; A-F is the same intuition
applied to the tier that is allowed to wait --- we do not migrate what is already running,
we schedule the deferrable tier into whichever replica has slack.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Related Work}

Iteration-level scheduling and its descendants frame the mechanism space: Orca introduced
iteration-level batching~\citep{orca}; Sarathi-Serve added chunked prefill and stall-free
batching, the token-budget structure vLLM V1 inherits~\citep{sarathi};
FastServe~\citep{fastserve} brought preemptive MLFQ scheduling to inference;
Llumnix~\citep{llumnix} reschedules across instances by live migration; DistServe and
Splitwise disaggregate prefill from decode~\citep{distserve,splitwise}, an architecture
ConServe found interacts poorly with offline co-location; InferCept's preemption
taxonomy~\citep{infercept} underlies HyGen's KV-handling choices. Co-location:
HyGen~\citep{hygen} (profiled latency budget, prefix-sharing offline order, starvation
tempering --- but no per-job deadline or admission contract), ConServe~\citep{conserve}
(context-aware latency model, layer-wise preemption, incremental KV checkpointing --- fork,
no contract), OOCO~\citep{ooco}, Echo~\citep{echo}. Deadline-aware serving:
QLM~\citep{qlm} (ILP queue reordering; evaluates EDF as a baseline it beats),
Ascendra~\citep{ascendra}, SCORPIO~\citep{scorpio}, and closest, Niyama~\citep{niyama} ---
per-request deadlines across QoS classes in one engine, with EDF/SRPF-interpolated priority
at second-to-minute horizons and relegation under overload; it does not expose per-job
day-scale windows, admission refusal, or laxity. FREESH~\citep{freesh} implements true LLF
in an LLM engine for carbon-aware frequency scaling over one request class --- evidence the
math runs in this setting, aimed at a different problem. Within vLLM: priority scheduling
was motivated by batch/interactive co-location~\citep{vllm-rfc-6077}, an SLA-tier proposal
was declined~\citep{vllm-rfc-30256}, and an online batch-API endpoint remains an open
PR~\citep{vllm-pr-44445} --- demand without an assembled answer. Every individual mechanism
here is published; the contribution is the contract semantics, the synthesis, and the
placement measurement.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Admission gating does not bound resident-request inflation} (technique B): a
resident batch request keeps decoding until the guardband evicts it, and where KV pressure
never materializes, eviction never fires --- the dominant residual interference on our
testbed. Bounding it requires per-iteration participation control (abandoning the wrapper's
upstream tolerance) or ConServe-style sub-iteration preemption. \textbf{The contract is
mechanism-validated, not outcome-demonstrated}: no tested regime discriminated escalation
end-to-end (\cref{sec:eval}, Q3), and the \code{sla\_strict} dial's latency price is
unmeasured. \textbf{Tail metrics flatter co-location}; we report distributions. \textbf{The
placement comparison is CPU-only}: \cref{sec:gpu} validates technique A on GPU, but the
A-vs-B inversion rests entirely on the CPU testbed. \textbf{Median tax}: even technique A
costs $\sim$2.5$\times$ p50 on the CPU box; co-location is not free and we do not claim it
is (on GPU the same tax is $1.18\times$, cross-node). \textbf{The GPU ceiling is a
steady-state tail, not a sustained dedicated run}: the \code{offline\_only} arm was lost to
the harness defect below, so the denominator is a 10\,s window of an 18.5\,s probe burst
rather than a 6-minute dedicated-offline run at the same window length as the co-serving
arms --- the right denominator of the two available, still not the one we wanted.
\textbf{GPU latency ratios are cross-node}: the \code{online\_only} baseline and the
technique-A run were measured on different physical A6000 nodes; the $\pm 0.62\%$
steady-state band across nodes suggests they are comparable, but no within-node pair
exists and we do not claim one. Single
engine, single model; one dispatcher per store (no lease); submission-time escalation cannot
re-age already-queued items; step-time attribution is exact on the CPU build and approximate
under GPU async scheduling; \code{scheduler\_cls} is not a stable interface (technique B pins
a version; technique A does not).

\paragraph{Known defects in the GPU harness.} We list these as defects rather than
caveats because they are ours, and they bound what \cref{sec:gpu} may be read to say.
(1) \textbf{Direct-submission arms fail at GPU scale} --- a two-stage defect: the salvage
fix for the original unbounded/hanging drain is verified in unit tests, but a second
stall-after-warmup mode remains, and it cost us the \code{offline\_only}, \code{naive} and
technique-B GPU arms. (2) \textbf{macOS fleet isolation is thread-count only}
(\cref{sec:widening}); shared memory bandwidth between the two replicas is unisolated and
is a named confound on the fleet latency numbers. (3) \textbf{Diurnal-B and B-matrix GPU
numbers are absent}, blocked by defect (1) --- so the placement inversion remains a
CPU-only result and the GPU column of the placement comparison is empty. (4) \textbf{The
metrics evidence is our own instrumentation, not a production observability stack}:
Grafana per-deployment dashboards do not exist on the dev cluster (Prometheus is behind a
VPN), so what we archive is the harness's own 1\,Hz scrapes plus archived CaaS API
responses and engine logs (all under \code{evidence/}). (5) \textbf{Two analysis defects,
found by an independent numerical audit after the first draft, not by us}: a
ramp-dominated probe average used as a ceiling, and a pipeline-fill bin left inside a
correlation. Both are corrected in \cref{sec:gpu} and in
\code{scripts/gpu\_analysis.py}; both had a visible tell (a $>$100\% harvest, a first bin
completing 771 items against 1667--1894) that we did not act on.

\section{Conclusion}

The batch API is the rare product where the entire margin is scheduling. Tidal implements
its contract --- admission, escalation, floor --- over an unmodified open-source engine with
classical real-time machinery, measures what each placement of that policy costs, and is
explicit about what a modest testbed can and cannot demonstrate. We offer it as usable
infrastructure, as a baseline for deadline-contracted serving, and as a placement study the
fork-based literature has not run --- with the GPU-scale evaluation as the necessary next
step.
